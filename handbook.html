The ConversiveDocs Workspace Handbook

This handbook explains, in plain language, how teams get the most out of ConversiveDocs. It is written to be read from start to finish, but each section also stands on its own, so you can jump to whatever is most useful to you today. Nothing here requires technical knowledge. If you can write an email, you can follow every part of this guide. The handbook is maintained by the ConversiveDocs Customer Success team and is reviewed every quarter. The current edition is Handbook Edition 1.0, published on October 8, 2026.

1. Why a shared workspace matters

Most teams do not lose knowledge because people stop writing things down. They lose knowledge because what gets written down ends up scattered across chat threads, personal notes, email attachments, and half-finished documents in a dozen different folders. When a new teammate joins, they spend their first weeks asking the same questions their predecessors asked, and the answers they receive depend on who happens to be online. A shared workspace changes this by giving every piece of team knowledge a single, findable home.

ConversiveDocs was built around a simple idea: the cost of writing something down should be lower than the cost of explaining it twice. When documentation is easy to create, easy to find, and easy to keep current, people actually use it. When any one of those three things is hard, documentation quietly decays until nobody trusts it. Everything in this handbook is aimed at keeping all three easy.

Internally we call this principle "write once, answer forever." A good page answers the same question for every future reader without anyone needing to be interrupted. Teams that adopt this habit typically report that the volume of repeated questions in their chat channels drops noticeably within the first two months, which frees senior staff to focus on work that genuinely needs their attention.

2. Setting up your first workspace

A workspace is the top-level container for everything your team writes. Most organisations need only one workspace, even if they have many departments, because ConversiveDocs uses spaces inside the workspace to separate teams. Creating multiple workspaces is only recommended when two groups must never see each other's content, such as a parent company and a client it serves under a strict confidentiality agreement.

When you create a workspace, you choose a name, an address, and a default language. The name can be changed at any time. The address, which forms part of every link your team shares, can be changed only once every thirty days, because changing it too often breaks bookmarks and links saved in other tools. The default language controls the interface and the language used for AI summaries, although each person can override it in their own profile settings.

After the workspace exists, the first thing we recommend is creating three spaces before inviting anyone: one for company-wide information such as policies and holidays, one for your own team, and one called Sandbox where people can experiment without worrying about cluttering real content. New members who have a safe place to practise tend to start contributing to real spaces much sooner than those who do not.

3. Spaces, pages, and the shape of good structure

Inside a workspace, content is organised into spaces, and inside each space, content is organised into pages that can be nested beneath one another. There is no hard limit on nesting depth, but in practice we advise keeping structures no more than four levels deep. Beyond that point, readers lose track of where they are and start relying entirely on search, which is fine for finding a single answer but poor for understanding how a topic fits together.

A helpful way to think about structure is to imagine a newcomer opening the space for the first time. The top-level pages should read like a table of contents for that team's work. Each one should have a short introduction explaining what lives beneath it and who looks after it. If you cannot write that introduction in two or three sentences, the section is probably trying to hold too many unrelated things and would benefit from being split.

Every page has an owner. By default the owner is the person who created the page, but ownership can be transferred to anyone with edit access. The owner receives a gentle reminder when a page has not been reviewed for one hundred and eighty days. This reminder is one of the most important features in the product, because stale pages are the main reason teams stop trusting their documentation. When an owner leaves the company, their pages are automatically reassigned to the space administrator so that nothing is orphaned.

4. Writing pages people actually read

The best documentation is written for a specific reader with a specific question. Before you start a page, it is worth asking yourself who will open it and what they will be trying to do at that moment. A page about expense claims, for example, is usually opened by someone who has a receipt in their hand and wants to know exactly what to do next. That reader does not need the history of the expense policy. They need the steps, the deadline, and the name of the person to contact if something goes wrong.

Put the answer first. Readers scan the opening lines of a page to decide whether they are in the right place, and many never scroll further. If the most important instruction is buried in the fourth paragraph, a large share of readers will miss it. Background, reasoning, and edge cases can follow afterwards for those who need them.

Use plain words and short sentences. Documentation is not the place to impress anyone with vocabulary. A sentence that a tired person can understand at the end of a long day is a good sentence. When you must use a technical term or an internal acronym, explain it the first time it appears on the page, even if you think everyone already knows it, because the people who do not know it are exactly the ones who need the page most.

Finally, date anything that is likely to change. A sentence such as "as of October 2026, the approval limit is five thousand dollars" ages far more gracefully than "the approval limit is currently five thousand dollars," because a future reader can immediately judge whether the information might be out of date.

5. Collaborating in real time

Several people can edit the same page at the same time. Each person's cursor appears in a distinct colour with their name beside it, so it is always clear who is working on which part. Changes appear for everyone within a fraction of a second, and there is no need to save, because every keystroke is stored automatically. If your connection drops, you can keep typing, and your edits will be merged as soon as you are back online.

When you want to suggest a change rather than make it directly, switch to suggestion mode. Your edits will appear as proposals that the page owner can accept or decline one by one. Suggestion mode is especially useful for policy pages, legal text, and anything customer-facing, where a second pair of eyes is valuable before a change goes live.

Comments can be attached to any word, sentence, or paragraph. A comment thread stays open until someone marks it resolved, and resolved threads remain visible in the page history for anyone who wants to understand why a decision was made. We encourage teams to resolve comments promptly. A page with dozens of open comments feels unfinished, and readers are less likely to trust it.

6. Finding information with Smart Search

Smart Search looks across every space you have permission to see and returns results ranked by relevance, freshness, and how often your colleagues have found a page useful. You do not need to remember exact titles or keywords. Typing a question in everyday language, such as "how do I request time off," usually produces better results than typing a single word.

Search respects permissions completely. If a page lives in a space you cannot access, it will never appear in your results, not even as a title. This means administrators can safely keep sensitive information in restricted spaces without worrying that search will leak its existence.

Search results also show a short AI-generated answer at the top when the workspace has enough relevant content to support one. This answer always cites the pages it was drawn from, and you can click through to read the original. If no page contains a confident answer, Smart Search says so rather than guessing. Administrators can see a weekly report of questions that returned no confident answer, which is one of the most useful signals for deciding what documentation to write next.

7. Permissions without the headache

ConversiveDocs uses four roles: Viewer, Commenter, Editor, and Administrator. Viewers can read. Commenters can read and leave comments. Editors can create and change pages. Administrators can do everything editors can do and can also manage members, roles, and space settings. Roles can be assigned at the workspace level or overridden for an individual space.

The simplest approach, and the one we recommend for most teams, is to give everyone Editor access to the company-wide space and their own team's space, and Viewer access to other teams' spaces. Openness by default encourages people to fix small mistakes when they see them, which keeps documentation healthy. Restrictions should be the exception, reserved for content such as salaries, legal matters, and unannounced plans.

Guests are people outside your organisation, such as contractors or clients. Guests can be invited to individual spaces only, never to the whole workspace, and their access can be set to expire automatically after seven, thirty, or ninety days. Guests never count towards your paid seat total, and every workspace may invite up to fifty active guests at no extra charge.

8. Keeping content fresh

Documentation does not stay accurate on its own. The single most effective habit a team can build is a short monthly review, where each page owner glances at the pages they look after and confirms they are still correct. ConversiveDocs supports this with a review queue that lists every page whose review reminder has fired. Marking a page as reviewed resets its reminder and adds a small "verified" label with the date, which tells readers that someone has recently checked it.

When a page is no longer needed, archive it rather than deleting it. Archived pages disappear from navigation and search but remain available in the archive for three years, after which they are permanently removed. Deleted pages, by contrast, go to the trash and are permanently removed after thirty days. Archiving is almost always the safer choice, because it is surprisingly common for an old page to become relevant again during an audit or an investigation.

Version history keeps a full record of every change made to a page for three hundred and sixty-five days. You can compare any two versions side by side and restore an earlier one with a single click. Restoring a version does not erase the history in between; it simply creates a new version that matches the old content, so nothing is ever lost.

9. Onboarding new teammates

A well-kept workspace is one of the best onboarding tools a company can have. We suggest every team maintains a page called "Start here" at the top of its space. This page should introduce the team, explain what it is responsible for, list the tools it uses, and link to the five or six pages a newcomer should read in their first week. Keeping this list short is important. A newcomer handed fifty links reads none of them carefully.

Pairing written onboarding with a real person works best. Assign each new teammate a buddy who has been on the team for at least six months. The buddy's job is not to repeat what the documentation already says, but to answer the questions the documentation does not yet cover, and then to make sure the answer gets written down. In this way, every new hire leaves the workspace a little better than they found it.
